\begin{abstract}
Oral defense (viva) in software engineering capstone projects is essential for assessing students' understanding of logic and codebase architecture, but manual assessment is labor-intensive and inconsistent. Conversely, off-the-shelf large language models (LLMs) often suffer from context hallucinations and evaluation biases. This paper presents PACE, an AI-assisted viva assessment system featuring complexity-aware hybrid context routing, a multi-layer guardrail architecture, and a role-decoupled Multi-LLM Ensemble to deliver reliable formative feedback. We evaluate this approach alongside three single-LLM feedback paradigms (\textit{Sandwich}, \textit{Rubric}, and \textit{Explainable}) in a randomized, student-blinded, within-subject study involving 344 rated feedback instances across 86 students. Results show high endorsement of the AI's technical accuracy and usefulness (79.9\% direct acceptance; 15-item AAI composite mean 3.99, 95\% CI [3.85, 4.13]), though student-reported learning motivation (41.9\%) and perceived argument persuasiveness (30.8\%) were considerably lower. Pairwise comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences in direct acceptance across the four feedback formats, while item-level analyses highlighted specific variations in actionability. These findings offer practical insights into student perceptions of guardrailed, multi-format AI feedback in collaborative software development education.

\keywords{Automated Viva Assessment \and Multi-LLM Ensemble \and Formative Feedback \and Pedagogical AI Guardrails \and Software Engineering Education.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Formative feedback is effective only when learners accurately interpret, evaluate, and act upon the guidance they receive~\cite{hattie2007power,shute2008focus,carless2018feedback,winstone2017supporting}. In automated environments, learners' willingness to accept and act on feedback depends on perceived usefulness~\cite{davis1989perceived,venkatesh2003user}, calibrated trust, and tendencies toward algorithm aversion or appreciation~\cite{lee2004trust,dietvorst2015algorithm,logg2019algorithm}. While large language models (LLMs) enable scalable formative feedback in computing education~\cite{kasneci2023chatgpt,denny2024computing,dai2023can}, general-purpose models remain prone to code-context hallucinations~\cite{hellas2023exploring,kiesler2023exploring}, prompt injection vulnerabilities~\cite{liffiton2023codehelp}, and uneven learner trust across accuracy, specificity, and evidentiary transparency~\cite{escalante2023ai,zhang2024students,nazaretsky2024ai,er2025assessing}.

Two technical and pedagogical gaps motivate this work. \textbf{Gap 1 (Automated Viva with Hybrid Context Routing):} Traditional input--output autograders cannot verify individual comprehension of collaborative, multi-language capstone repositories; although automated program questioning has been explored for small programs~\cite{lehtinen2021lets}, scenario-based oral defenses (vivas) integrating static call-graph extraction with complexity-aware context routing remain underexplored. \textbf{Gap 2 (Feedback Configurations and Single-Model Sycophancy):} Single-model evaluators (\textit{LLM-as-a-Judge}) exhibit sycophancy and grading bias~\cite{zheng2023judging}. While distinct formative paradigms—\textit{Sandwich}~\cite{parkes2013feedback}, \textit{Rubric}~\cite{jonsson2007use}, \textit{Explainable}~\cite{khosravi2022explainable}, and role-decoupled \textit{Multi-LLM Ensembles}~\cite{du2024improving,zheng2023judging}—offer complementary pedagogical mechanisms, randomized within-subject evaluations of these configurations on students' own capstone codebases remain scarce.

To address these gaps, we present \textbf{PACE} (\textit{Project Assistance and Course Evaluation}), an AI-assisted viva system deployed at FPT University. PACE parses C\#/.NET (Roslyn) and Java, JavaScript, and TypeScript (tree-sitter) repositories, routing business functions with $N_w \le 10$ call-graph nodes to raw source code (\texttt{Raw}) and those with $N_w > 10$ to Mermaid control/data-flow graphs (\texttt{Graph}) for scenario question generation via OpenAI \texttt{gpt-6-luna}. A three-layer guardrail pipeline with XML delimiter sandboxing secures evaluation across three single-LLM formats (\textit{Sandwich}, \textit{Rubric}, \textit{Explainable}; DeepSeek-V3) and a two-stage \textit{Researching} ensemble chaining DeepSeek-V3 (\textit{Code Inspector}) with Groq \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b} (\textit{Pedagogical Synthesizer and Rubric Grader}).

In a randomized, student-blinded, within-subject study, 86 students answered four scenario questions on their team repositories and rated each feedback configuration on a 15-item, unidirectionally keyed Likert instrument grounded in FPQ, XAI, argument persuasiveness, psychological safety, and TAM/UTAUT frameworks~\cite{cronbach1951coefficient,hayes2020use,podsakoff2003common,kim2019straightlining,vansonderen2013ineffectiveness}. We investigate three research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How do students evaluate the content quality, perceived usefulness, and overall acceptance of LLM-generated feedback on viva responses about their own capstone project codebases?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How do students' agreement rates and multidomain ratings differ across the four feedback formats (\textit{Sandwich}, \textit{Rubric}, \textit{Explainable}, and \textit{Researching}/\textit{Multi-LLM Ensemble}) based on non-parametric tests and pairwise comparisons with corrections for multiple testing~\cite{friedman1937use,holm1979simple,lakens2017equivalence}?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] To what extent are student acceptance and perceived feedback quality associated with the use of raw source-code contexts ($N_w \leq 10$) versus Mermaid flow-graph contexts ($N_w > 10$) in the deployed system?
\end{itemize}

This work contributes an integrated, guardrailed viva assessment architecture and a controlled within-subject evaluation extending automated program questioning~\cite{lehtinen2021lets} to collaborative capstone repositories, distinguishing agreement with AI judgments from actionability, motivation, and future-use intention. Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} details the PACE architecture; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} describes the experimental design; Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses findings and threats to validity (Section~\ref{sec:threats}); and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}\label{sec:related_work}

This work draws on three main research areas: automated formative feedback in computing education, student acceptance and perceptions of AI-generated guidance, and pedagogical feedback configurations in software engineering assessment.

\subsection{LLM-Generated Feedback in Programming Education}

Automated assessment in computing education predates the emergence of generative AI, with early diagnostic tools focusing primarily on static error detection rather than actionable next steps~\cite{keuning2019automated}. While recent evaluations demonstrate that off-the-shelf LLMs can effectively identify issues in code, they remain susceptible to hallucinating nonexistent defects or suggesting misleading fixes~\cite{hellas2023exploring,kiesler2023exploring,dai2023can}. To address these safety and reliability concerns, specialized systems such as \textit{CodeHelp} have demonstrated the potential of incorporating guardrails to provide scalable programming assistance without directly supplying complete solutions~\cite{liffiton2023codehelp}. Building on these foundations, our approach shifts the evaluation focus from isolated, beginner-level coding snippets to comprehensive explanations of business logic and system architecture across evolving, multi-language team repositories.

\subsection{Student Perceptions and Acceptance of AI Feedback}

Understanding how learners respond to automated feedback requires examining established technology acceptance and trust frameworks. According to the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and UTAUT, perceived usefulness and ease of use are important predictors of technology acceptance and use~\cite{davis1989perceived,venkatesh2003user}, whereas appropriate reliance on automation requires calibrating trust to actual system reliability~\cite{lee2004trust}. Psychological phenomena such as algorithm aversion, in which users prematurely abandon algorithmic recommendations after observing errors, coexist with instances of algorithm appreciation~\cite{dietvorst2015algorithm,logg2019algorithm}. In computing education, empirical studies have revealed diverse learner preferences and perceptions: Zhang et al.\ documented student preferences for feedback that explicitly references source code~\cite{zhang2024students}, Er et al.\ identified continued advantages of human instructor feedback~\cite{er2025assessing}, and Nazaretsky et al.\ demonstrated that student trust is sensitive to the perceived source of AI-generated feedback~\cite{nazaretsky2024ai}. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating multiple dimensions of learner perception, including acceptance, behavioral intention, trust, and affective responses, alongside technical correctness.

\subsection{Pedagogical Feedback Configurations and Multi-Model Orchestration}

Different formats of formative feedback are grounded in distinct pedagogical principles. Sandwich feedback (\textit{Sandwich}), which places constructive criticism between supportive remarks, has been shown in controlled studies to improve learners' affective responses, even when performance gains remain modest~\cite{parkes2013feedback}. Explicit scoring rubrics (\textit{Rubric}) establish transparent evaluation criteria and promote grading consistency~\cite{jonsson2007use}, while evidence-based explanations (\textit{Explainable}) can support appropriate trust calibration by providing transparent references to source code~\cite{khosravi2022explainable}. To mitigate grading biases and sycophancy observed in LLM-based evaluations (\textit{LLM-as-a-Judge})~\cite{zheng2023judging}, multi-agent debate and multi-model coordination architectures have been proposed to improve factual accuracy and objectivity in reasoning~\cite{du2024improving,zheng2023judging}. However, existing literature offers limited evidence from rigorous, randomized within-subject evaluations comparing these distinct formative feedback approaches, particularly when implemented through role-decoupled multi-model ensembles operating on authentic student capstone codebases.

\paragraph{Position Relative to Prior Literature.}

While prior work has used automated comprehension checks to assess program understanding~\cite{lehtinen2021lets}, our work extends this approach to a structured viva defense preparation workflow involving collaborative team repositories. Unlike studies focusing on isolated CS1 programming tasks or hypothetical model-generated code snippets~\cite{zhang2024students,lehtinen2024askai}, PACE investigates student perceptions of multiple AI feedback formats in the context of authentic, multi-language capstone projects. Furthermore, by implementing a complexity-aware hybrid context router ($\tau = 10$), a three-layer guardrail architecture with XML delimiter sandboxing, and a role-decoupled Multi-LLM Ensemble, this study provides an empirical evaluation of guardrailed, multi-format formative feedback in software engineering education.

\subsection{Multi-Language Static Analysis and Complexity-Aware Context Routing}

For each submitted team repository, PACE clones the target branch and parses C\#/.NET source code via Roslyn and Java (Spring Boot), JavaScript, and TypeScript (Express, NestJS, Hono) via tree-sitter. Extracted API endpoints and controllers are grouped into user-facing \emph{business functions} (e.g., ``budget management'' or ``asset auction'') via metadata analysis using Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, subject to instructor review. To select an appropriate context representation, PACE computes the number of distinct function nodes $N_w$ in each business function's call graph. A complexity-aware hybrid router retrieves raw source code (\texttt{Raw}) when $N_w \le 10$ to preserve fine-grained conditional expressions, and routes functions with $N_w > 10$ to a Mermaid control- and data-flow graph (\texttt{Graph}) encoding ordered edges, branch conditions, and return types to reduce token overhead and syntactic noise. Scenario-based questions are generated using OpenAI \texttt{gpt-6-luna} (temperature 0.3, strict JSON schema) under a business-language-only constraint (excluding implementation terms such as \emph{Controller}, \emph{DTO}, or \emph{SQL}) across three exception-branch difficulty levels: Happy Path, Single Exception, and Double Exception.

\subsection{Guardrailed Multi-Format Evaluation Engine and Multi-LLM Ensemble}

When a student submits $k = 4$ scenario answers across distinct business functions, PACE executes an atomic database transaction ($<200$ ms) with row-level locking (\texttt{SELECT FOR UPDATE}) before invoking external LLMs. Submissions pass through a three-layer guardrail pipeline: (1) an \emph{Input Guardrail} applying 17 bilingual (English/Vietnamese) blacklist checks against prompt injection and jailbreak attempts; (2) an \emph{Execution Guardrail} blocking malicious scripts and binary payloads via compiled regular expressions while sanitizing Unicode control characters; and (3) an \emph{Output Guardrail} enforcing strict JSON schemas and preventing API key leakage. To mitigate indirect prompt injection and enforce instruction hierarchy, inputs are sandboxed within four XML delimiter tags: \texttt{<ground\_truth\_context>} (raw code or Mermaid graph), \texttt{<exam\_question>}, \texttt{<suggested\_answer>}, and \texttt{<student\_answer>}.

Each student's four responses are assigned to four feedback configurations via random permutation without replacement ($\mathcal{F} = \{\text{Sandwich},$ $\text{Rubric},$ $\text{Explainable},$ $\text{Researching}\}$). Three single-LLM formats are generated by DeepSeek-V3 (\texttt{deepseek-chat}, temperature 0.2): \textbf{Sandwich} structures feedback into positive reinforcement, constructive criticism, and actionable next steps; \textbf{Rubric} provides quantitative competency scores on a five-point scale with analytical commentary; and \textbf{Explainable} grounds feedback in explicit source-code citations (\texttt{code\_evidence}) and logical trace explanations (\texttt{logic\_explanation}). The fourth configuration, \textbf{Researching (Multi-LLM Ensemble)}, employs a role-decoupled two-stage pipeline to mitigate single-model sycophancy: Stage~1 uses DeepSeek-V3 as an objective \textit{Code Inspector} to extract code evidence and logical discrepancies, and Stage~2 uses Groq (\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b}) as a \textit{Pedagogical Synthesizer and Rubric Grader} to synthesize formative feedback and rubric scores, supported by exponential backoff for HTTP~429 rate limits and single-model fallback.

\subsection{Empirical Data Collection and Survey Instrumentation}

Immediately after receiving each feedback instance, students rate it via a web interface on a 15-item, unidirectionally keyed five-point Likert scale ($1 = \text{Strongly disagree}$ to $5 = \text{Strongly agree}$) alongside optional free-text comments. The export pipeline automatically filters out load-testing accounts (MSSV prefixes starting with \texttt{K6}) to retain only verified student submissions.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:method}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Context and Participants}

The empirical evaluation was conducted in October 2026 in undergraduate software engineering capstone courses at FPT University, Vietnam. The research team invited final-year software engineering students to use PACE to prepare for their oral defenses (vivas), allowing them to practice explaining their teams' collaborative codebases. Participation was entirely voluntary, and students were explicitly assured that declining to participate would not affect their academic standing.

The resulting dataset comprises responses from 86 undergraduate students working across 28 distinct project repositories. Collectively, they submitted 344 scenario-based viva answers ($k = 4$ responses per student, each addressing a different business function). The answers were written in Vietnamese, with an average length of approximately 51 whitespace-delimited words per response, and were collected between October 20 and October 26, 2026.

\subsection{Survey Instrumentation}

After submitting each answer and receiving AI-generated feedback, students evaluated the corresponding feedback instance through a web interface using a 15-item questionnaire with a five-point Likert response scale ($1 = \text{Strongly disagree}$ to $5 = \text{Strongly agree}$). All fifteen items were positively keyed, with higher scores indicating more favorable perceptions. Drawing on established principles of formative feedback~\cite{shute2008focus}, technology acceptance frameworks (TAM/UTAUT)~\cite{davis1989perceived,venkatesh2003user}, trust in automation~\cite{lee2004trust}, argument persuasiveness, and psychological safety, the instrument assesses six conceptual constructs, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:instrument}.

\subsection{Dataset and Experimental Procedure}

The final dataset contains 344 complete feedback-rating records from 86 students, each of whom submitted one set of four answers. Every student evaluated all four feedback formats (\textit{Sandwich}, \textit{Rubric}, \textit{Explainable}, and \textit{Researching}), which were randomly assigned to their four answers without replacement. This design yielded 86 complete, balanced within-student blocks, with 86 rating instances per configuration.

Regarding the distribution of question contexts, 280 instances (corresponding to 157 unique questions) used Mermaid flow-graph contexts (\texttt{Graph}), whereas 64 instances (corresponding to 33 unique questions across 13 repositories) used raw source-code contexts (\texttt{Raw}). Thirty-two students encountered at least one raw-code question, and 26 of these students answered questions under both context types, enabling within-student comparisons across context types.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Methods}

To address our research questions while accounting for dependencies among observations from students, questions, and shared project repositories, we conducted statistical analyses using Python (\texttt{statsmodels})~\cite{seabold2010statsmodels}:

\paragraph{Scores and Composite Summaries.}
We calculated an exploratory multidomain composite, termed the \emph{AI Agreement Index} (AAI), as the unweighted mean across all fifteen positively keyed items (assigning equal weight to each of the 15 items rather than weighting conceptual domains equally). Individual items, such as \texttt{ACCEPTANCE}, were analyzed as direct measures of specific perceptions.

\paragraph{RQ1 (Perceptions and Drivers).}
To examine contemporaneous within-student associations with acceptance and behavioral intention, we fitted student fixed-effects linear regression models by demeaning predictors and outcomes within each student:
\begin{equation}
 y_{ij}-\bar y_j = \sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta_k(z_{kij}-\bar z_{kj}) + \sum_{c=2}^{4}\gamma_c(D_{cij}-\bar D_{cj}) + e_{ij},
 \label{eq:drivers}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes feedback instances, $j$ indexes students, $y$ denotes the acceptance or behavioral intention outcome, and $D_c$ represents feedback configuration indicators, with \textit{Sandwich} as the reference category. Predictors (perceived quality, utility, trust, affect, and safety) were standardized using full-sample standard deviations before within-student demeaning.

\paragraph{RQ2 (Feedback Configuration Comparisons).}
Primary comparisons focused on paired contrasts among the four feedback formats. To account for clustering within shared repositories, we reported repository-level bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples with replacement. We supplemented these analyses with exploratory nonparametric Friedman tests~\cite{friedman1937use}, Kendall's $W$, and Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{holm1979simple}. To control the family-wise error rate across all 90 pairwise item-level contrasts ($6$ format pairs $\times$ $15$ survey items), we constructed simultaneous 95\% sensitivity bands using a centered, bootstrap-standardized maximum statistic. For AAI scores, we assessed equivalence using the two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure with equivalence margins of $\pm 0.25$, $\pm 0.20$, and $\pm 0.10$~\cite{lakens2017equivalence}.

\paragraph{RQ3 (Context Type Association).}
Because context type was determined by the complexity-aware router ($\tau = 10$) rather than randomly assigned, it was associated with repository complexity. To examine these contextual associations, we fitted exploratory student fixed-effects models incorporating context-type and feedback-configuration indicators, with uncertainty estimated using 5,000 cluster-level bootstrap resamples. We further assessed sensitivity to repository clustering by iteratively excluding one repository at a time and recomputing the point estimates.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Reliability and Data Quality}
\label{sec:quality}

The fifteen positively keyed survey items showed high internal consistency ($\alpha = 0.916$, $\omega_t = 0.930$). Internal consistency estimates for the item groups were $\alpha = 0.91$ for information and pedagogical quality, $0.78$ for usefulness and actionability, $0.80$ for trust, reliability, and fairness, and $0.78$ for acceptance and behavioral intention. Affect and psychological safety showed lower internal consistency ($\alpha = 0.56$), providing limited support for treating these two items as a single unidimensional construct. However, internal consistency alone does not establish the validity of a multidimensional factor structure.

To examine potential response-pattern anomalies, we identified 64 instances (18.6\%) exhibiting straight-lining, in which students assigned identical ratings to all fifteen items. These included 11 students (12.8\%) who exhibited straight-lining across all four feedback instances. After excluding these flagged responses, the pooled internal consistency estimate was $\alpha = 0.90$. However, excluding responses based on this pattern alone does not establish whether the remaining responses were attentive. Because all fifteen items were positively keyed, with reverse-worded items omitted to minimize linguistic confusion~\cite{vansonderen2013ineffectiveness}, these pooled response-pattern statistics describe properties of the response distributions rather than providing psychometric validation or supporting inference based on independent observations.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Acceptance, Persuasiveness, Motivation, and Future-Use Intention.}
Across the survey responses, agreement with statements concerning technical accuracy, perceived usefulness, and direct acceptance of the AI's judgment (\texttt{ACCEPTANCE}, $79.9\%$) was considerably higher than agreement with statements concerning the persuasiveness of the AI's arguments (\texttt{PERSUASIVENESS}, $30.8\%$), learning motivation (\texttt{AFFECT}, $41.9\%$), and future-use intention (\texttt{BEHAVIORAL INTENTION}, $68.0\%$); the present data do not establish the cause of the divergence between accepting the AI's judgment and finding its reasoning persuasive. Student fixed-effects regressions indicated that perceived utility was positively associated with acceptance and future-use intention, while trust was positively associated with acceptance. These findings are consistent with established technology acceptance and trust in automation frameworks~\cite{davis1989perceived,venkatesh2003user,lee2004trust}. Notably, motivation was positively associated with willingness to revise after adjustment for psychological safety and other covariates, suggesting that different dimensions of learner affect may be associated with different outcomes. However, these self-reported perceptions do not establish actual code revision behavior or objective improvements in learning outcomes. Appropriate reliance on AI feedback therefore requires further evaluation against independent measures of system reliability~\cite{lee2004trust}.

\paragraph{Configuration Bundles and Pedagogical Design.}
Although mean AAI scores differed relatively little across the four formative feedback configurations, item-level results revealed more pronounced differences. For example, a mean actionability difference of approximately $0.37$ points was observed between the \textit{Sandwich} and \textit{Explainable} configurations. Configurations incorporating explicit next steps (\textit{Sandwich} and \textit{Researching}) received higher actionability ratings. However, these configurations also differed in feedback structure, scoring criteria, response length, and model orchestration. Consequently, the observed differences cannot be attributed to any single pedagogical component. Future controlled experiments should independently manipulate these components to identify their individual contributions. Furthermore, the role-decoupled \textit{Researching} configuration generated substantially longer explanations without clear evidence of an AAI advantage over the single-model \textit{Sandwich} configuration. This finding highlights the need to assess computational efficiency and cost-effectiveness using actual API token usage and cost records. Finally, the high student-level intraclass correlation ($\text{ICC} = .77$) underscores the importance of accounting for individual differences in baseline ratings when evaluating educational feedback systems.

\paragraph{Response Patterns and Measurement Challenges.}
The identification of straight-lining in 18.6\% of feedback-rating instances highlights potential measurement challenges when students evaluate multiple feedback instances consecutively using multi-item questionnaires. Reverse-worded items were omitted to reduce potential linguistic confusion~\cite{vansonderen2013ineffectiveness}; however, the extent to which this design choice affected response quality was not directly assessed. Response fatigue also remains a potential concern when students evaluate lengthy feedback across multiple criteria~\cite{kim2019straightlining}. Future deployments should consider shorter, targeted questionnaires, embedded attention checks, and behavioral interaction logs to better distinguish attentive engagement from potentially biased response patterns.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Internal validity:} Because each feedback configuration was applied to a distinct student answer addressing a different scenario question, confounding effects from question difficulty, cognitive load, and order cannot be ruled out despite random permutation; for RQ3, the complexity router ($\tau = 10$) confounds business-function complexity with context representation, and database logs recorded configuration assignments without independent provider-side execution telemetry. \textbf{Construct validity:} Ratings reflect unvalidated self-reports rather than expert-verified correctness, actual code revisions, or longitudinal learning gains; the secondary AAI averages the 15 items with equal item weights rather than weighting conceptual domains equally, pooled reliability combines within- and between-student variance, and concurrent self-reports risk common-method bias~\cite{podsakoff2003common}. \textbf{External validity:} Voluntary participation within a single institution (FPT University) for capstone viva preparation may limit generalizability to mandatory assessments, introductory courses, or other repository ecosystems and LLM backbones. \textbf{Statistical conclusion validity:} With 28 repository clusters, percentile-bootstrap confidence intervals remain approximate, fixed-effects regressions capture contemporaneous associations rather than causal effects, and the unvalidated $\pm 0.25$ AAI equivalence margin supports equivalence only within that statistical threshold.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

PACE integrates automated analysis of multi-language code repositories, complexity-aware hybrid context routing, guardrailed evaluation pipelines, and structured formative feedback into a collaborative capstone viva preparation workflow. In a field evaluation involving 344 rated feedback instances from 86 students, we found that ratings of perceived technical accuracy and usefulness were considerably higher than those of learning motivation ($41.9\%$) and future-use intention ($68.0\%$), and that direct agreement with the AI's judgment ($79.9\%$) was substantially higher than finding the AI's arguments persuasive ($30.8\%$); the underlying causes of this difference are not inferred from the present data. Pairwise comparisons of direct acceptance ratings revealed no statistically significant differences among the four feedback configurations. Exploratory equivalence testing supported equivalence in average composite scores within the selected $\pm 0.25$ margin, while item-level analyses revealed differences in specific feedback dimensions, including higher actionability ratings for certain configurations.

This study contributes an empirical field evaluation of multidimensional student perceptions of guardrailed, multi-format AI feedback in authentic collaborative software development settings. Future research should validate the psychometric properties of the survey instrument, assess technical correctness against independent expert evaluations, examine actual revision behaviors, and isolate the effects of individual feedback components through controlled experiments. Future deployments should also maintain versioned execution logs and systematically document institutional research ethics procedures.
